1       Summary

Reflectance describes how a surface reflects light at different wavelengths. SpectralBridge is Python
research software that helps teams prepare and check reflectance products from NEON airborne
sensors, drones, and Landsat. It retains named intermediate rasters, extracted values, quality
checks, and provenance so users can inspect how each product was made. Separate routes preserve
sensor-specific operations, while collection tools summarize selected flightlines and sites. The package
organizes established corrections rather than proposing a new algorithm. Tests and team research
examples demonstrate software contracts and limitations, not agreement among sensors.


2       Statement of need

Band names invite direct comparison, but spectral response, footprint, viewing direction, and
illumination help define the reflectance recorded by each instrument. A drone may isolate sunlit
foliage; NEON records a dense spectrum; Landsat mixes canopy, gaps, soil, and shadow across
broader pixels (Kampe et al., 2010; National Ecological Observatory Network, 2026; Woodcock

--- Source page 2 ---
and Strahler, 1987). Matching labels therefore do not make measurements equivalent, and these
differences can change an ecological interpretation (Nicodemus et al., 1977; Schaepman-Strub et al.,
2006; Teillet et al., 1997; Roy et al., 2016; Huang et al., 2002).
The intended users are remote-sensing researchers and developers who need to construct sensor-
oriented products, inspect intermediate states, verify outputs, or summarize flightlines. They remain
responsible for temporal matching, co-registration, and common spatial support. Independent
validation also requires characterized reference measurements and uncertainty analysis; process
completion alone cannot establish it (Origo et al., 2020).
SpectralBridge preserves the sequence from source through configured transformations to extracted
values, helping users locate where an unexpected pattern first appears. It does not diagnose
discrepancies or choose a valid spatial/temporal design. Figure 1 illustrates the separate roles of
spatial support and spectral response.

3              State of the field

HyTools is SpectralBridge’s closest methodological relative and part of its NEON lineage. HLS
and Sen2Like harmonize satellite products; FORCE processes satellite archives; Snakemake and
Nextflow orchestrate general pipelines (Chlus et al., 2025; Claverie et al., 2018; Ju et al., 2025;

--- Source page 4 ---
Saunier et al., 2022; Frantz, 2019; Köster and Rahmann, 2012; Di Tommaso et al., 2017). In the
systems compared here, none combines the separate NEON and drone routes with collection-level
artifact and QA contracts (Earth Lab, 2026x; Earth Lab, 2026j). SpectralBridge’s contribution is
this sensor-aware integration, restart checks, and grouped summaries—not a new correction method
or harmonization product.


4    Software design

The workflow addresses geometry normalization and target-band weighting, while preserving geospa-
tial products for downstream spatial reconciliation. It does not create a common spatial support;
nominally matching bands may also require bandpass adjustment (Chander et al., 2013; Roy et al.,
2016; Earth Lab, 2026z; Earth Lab, 2026m).
NEON provides the dense spectrum from which configured MicaSense- and Landsat-like products
are constructed. Independently acquired drone data follow a separate preparation path (National
Ecological Observatory Network, 2026; Earth Lab, 2026z; Earth Lab, 2026h). Thus, Drone →
NEON → Landsat describes a comparison design, not a physical conversion chain. A named target
product reflects configured weighting and any recorded adjustment, while retaining NEON’s original
spatial support.


Geometry and terrain

For NEON, the workflow estimates bandwise SCS+C topographic terms, then applies RossThick
and LiDenseR BRDF terms using available solar and view geometry (Queally et al., 2022; Earth
Lab, 2026r; Earth Lab, 2026ab). These are established methods, with code lineage to HyTools
acknowledged in the implementation (Ross, 1981; Roujean et al., 1992; Li and Strahler, 1992; Soenen
et al., 2005; Wanner et al., 1995; Schaaf et al., 2002; Chlus et al., 2025). Invalid geometry ratios
receive neutral factors, which QA should report. Both corrections share one output cube, so their
separate effects cannot be isolated from that artifact.


Spectral response

Different instruments weight different parts of the spectrum, so matching band names do not
guarantee matching measurements (Chander et al., 2013; Roy et al., 2016). Given source reflectance
samples ρi and target-band weights sb,i sampled on the same source-band grid, SpectralBridge

The implementation normalizes supplied response weights by their discrete sum; it does not perform
wavelength-interval quadrature. Weights must therefore match the source grid and intended spectral
support. Actual Landsat weighting requires measured relative spectral responses, not band centers
and widths alone (Barsi et al., 2014). A deterministic fixture checks this arithmetic to an absolute
tolerance of 2 × 10−7 (Earth Lab, 2026s; Earth Lab, 2026ae).
Source atmospheric correction is inherited from NEON; some configured Landsat-like products
also receive a separate empirical brightness adjustment (National Ecological Observatory Network,

--- Source page 5 ---
2026; Earth Lab, 2026l). These configured representations are not proof of physical agreement with
another sensor (Claverie, 2023; Ju et al., 2025).


Spatial support

Matching band spaces does not make a crown-scale spectrum and a Landsat pixel the same spatial
mixture (Teillet et al., 1997; Woodcock and Strahler, 1987; Huang et al., 2002). SpectralBridge
preserves georeferenced rasters and polygon extractions but leaves grid alignment, overlap, and
aggregation to the user, because these choices determine which observations contribute (Dungan
et al., 2002; Earth Lab, 2026m; Earth Lab, 2026h).


Architecture and data flow

Figure 2 aligns the two observation routes and the shared collection-analysis path. NEON and
drone processing share most stages; their main differences are at the edges: drone processing
adds local-image staging and may use an affine mapping, while NEON’s dense spectrum supports
target-band convolution.
Each route writes named artifacts for distinct measurement states, linking the source to corrected
and target-band products (Earth Lab, 2026e; Earth Lab, 2026m).

The architecture evolved from a HyTools-based workflow; HyTools is no longer a runtime dependency,
but lineage remains in NEON access, ENVI writing, and correction components (Chlus et al., 2025;
Earth Lab, 2025a; Earth Lab, 2025b; Earth Lab, 2026i; Earth Lab, 2026t; Earth Lab, 2026r).
The NEON route applies configured geometry corrections and spectral weighting to its source cube.
The drone route retains native bands and may apply reviewed affine coefficients, without runtime
coefficient fitting or spectral convolution (Earth Lab, 2026e; Earth Lab, 2026m; Earth Lab, 2026h;
Earth Lab, 2026c). Collection analysis streams aligned ENVI windows into pooled, flightline-,
and site-level fits. It accumulates compact statistics but still reads source rasters; no performance
advantage is claimed without a matched benchmark (Earth Lab, 2026a; Earth Lab, 2026aa; Earth
Lab, 2026b).
Named files serve as stage interfaces, not disposable cache. This design lets users inspect intermediate
states, check stage-specific QA, and reuse valid products after interruption; it costs storage and
I/O, and does not serialize every realized parameter. Restart therefore supports recovery, not

--- Source page 7 ---
guaranteed exact replay. Completion is request-specific: every configured output must exist and be
readable (Earth Lab, 2026j; Earth Lab, 2026d; Earth Lab, 2026ad; Earth Lab, 2026u).

Figure 3: Four questions govern a multi-stage scientific workflow. Normal process termination does
not establish production of the requested scientific state; artifact existence does not establish QA;
and QA does not establish agreement with independent observations. Remote-sensing validation
frameworks likewise treat validation as staged evidence rather than a binary declaration (Morisette
et al., 2006; Origo et al., 2020). The examples in the Research impact statement illustrate these
separate checks (SpectralBridge project team, 2026; Earth Lab, 2026p).


The package is OS-independent, requires Python 3.10 or newer, and is distributed under the
GNU General Public License version 3 (GPLv3); installation and usage are documented in its
README (Earth Lab, 2026v; Earth Lab, 2026x). The evidence spans version 2.2.0 at f7e7ddd for
the historical NEON route and c86da6a for later bulk work and 2.3.0rc1 preparation, but supplied
run records do not link every result to an exact revision (Earth Lab, 2026y; Earth Lab, 2026g). The
public release page listed v1.0.0 at commit 9d1de28 as latest on 12 October 2026; the cited Zenodo
DOI is a pre-rename archive, not a record for these results (Earth Lab, 2026w; Earth Lab, 2026f).
The submitted source, evidence, and permanent archive citation must identify a consistent version.


Reuse potential

Reuse is most direct for NEON products and drone observations that match the supported input
schemas. Users can configure target-band responses or reviewed affine registries, while preserving
exact wavelength definitions and coefficient provenance; adding spatial aggregation or a new sensor
remains a study-specific extension. The ENVI, Parquet, and JSON products can then be inspected
or consumed by downstream analysis tools (Earth Lab, 2026m; Earth Lab, 2026h; Earth Lab,
2026x).


5    Research impact statement

The examples below come from the authors’ ongoing research workflow for comparing NEON, drone,
and Landsat reflectance; they are developer-team applications, not evidence of external adoption.
Deterministic fixtures cover convolution, correction, extraction, QA, restart, and collection-analysis
contracts (Kanewala and Bieman, 2014; Earth Lab, 2026ac; Earth Lab, 2026n). In the R10C

--- Source page 8 ---
NEON example, the declared route produced 18 readable Parquet outputs, while QA retained
warnings, including 68 poor-quality wavelength bands that were reported but not masked. This
demonstrates artifact completion and warning preservation, not correction efficacy or agreement
with Landsat (Earth Lab, 2026p; Earth Lab, 2026q).
A separate 43-flight drone attempt exposes the value of explicit completion checks: orchestration
labeled all flights processed, but required ENVI and Parquet outputs were absent (SpectralBridge
project team, 2026). A supplied bulk analysis summarizes 122 flightlines, but its executed software
revision and run manifest were not provided (SpectralBridge bulk evidence bundle 2026; Earth Lab,
2026o). Together, these cases show how the workflow can expose incomplete production, preserve
warnings, and summarize collections while making the limits of the evidence visible. They do not
establish independent cross-sensor accuracy, a performance advantage, improved ecological inference,
or adoption beyond the developer team.
